AI-Enabled One Health Early Warning System for Emerging Antimicrobial Resistance
Problem and Background
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global public-health challenge. The misuse and overuse of antibiotics have contributed to the development and spread of resistant pathogens, making some infections increasingly difficult to treat and placing greater burdens on patients, healthcare providers, and health systems (World Health Organization, 2026).
Surveillance is essential for identifying emerging resistance and supporting timely public-health responses. However, AMR is not confined to human clinical settings. Resistant pathogens and resistance genes can circulate across interconnected human, animal, food, and environmental systems, making AMR an important One Health challenge (Martak et al., 2024). Yet existing surveillance still relies heavily on human clinical data, which may limit the ability to identify earlier signals emerging from other sectors.
This project therefore aims to develop and evaluate a proof-of-concept AI early-warning framework that integrates surveillance data across One Health domains to provide a more comprehensive picture of AMR risk.
Research Question:
Can One Health surveillance data from human, animal, food, and environmental sources be integrated into an AI system to detect and warn of emerging antimicrobial-resistance signals earlier than conventional surveillance methods?
Target Users:
Public health researchers, medical professionals, public health agencies, and policymakers.
Objectives
The project has three main objectives:
1. To develop a proof-of-concept AI early-warning system that integrates human, animal, food, and environmental AMR surveillance data.
2. To evaluate whether cross-sector One Health signals can detect emerging AMR earlier than conventional clinical-only surveillance.
3. To assess the contribution of different surveillance sectors and provide interpretable alerts to support public-health investigation and decision-making.
Literature Review
AMR is a major global public-health challenge, with bacterial AMR associated with approximately 4.71 million deaths worldwide in 2021 (GBD 2021 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2024). Because AMR spreads across human, animal, food, and environmental systems, One Health surveillance has become increasingly important (Martak et al., 2024; Delpy et al., 2024). AI has also shown potential for AMR prediction using clinical, genomic, and hospital-level data (Yelin et al., 2019; Ren et al., 2022; Vihta et al., 2024).
However, existing AI-based AMR systems still face challenges in data heterogeneity, generalizability, and interpretability, and most studies focus on individual data sources rather than integrated cross-sector surveillance (Elalouf et al., 2025; Delpy et al., 2024). This creates a gap in understanding whether AI-enabled One Health integration can provide earlier warning of emerging AMR than conventional human-health surveillance alone.
Methodology
To provide earlier warning of emerging antimicrobial resistance (AMR), we will design and evaluate the Antimicrobial Resistance One Health Early Warning System (AMR-OHEWS). For each resistance determinant in each region, the system will estimate every week the probability that the determinant will become established in humans within a defined forecast period.
To identify signals before they become visible in clinical surveillance, we will combine information from human, animal and veterinary, food-chain, and wastewater sources (Aarestrup & Woolhouse, 2020; Larsson & Flach, 2022; Woolhouse et al., 2015). The data will be aligned by region, week, and resistance determinant. Reporting delays will be applied before features are constructed so that every prediction uses only information that would have been available at that time.
To determine whether cross-sector information improves early warning, we will compare conventional clinical-only surveillance methods (Farrington et al., 1996; Salmon et al., 2016) with three cross-sector approaches: a gradient-boosted classifier, a temporal neural network (Bai et al., 2018), and a graph-based model that represents connections between regions (Hamilton et al., 2017; Kipf & Welling, 2017). Their predictions will also be combined in an ensemble. Clinical-only variants and sector-ablation analyses will help distinguish the value of additional data from the effect of model complexity.
To measure warning time against known emergence events, we will first evaluate the models using simulated surveillance data. To assess real-world relevance, we will then use observed surveillance data to test the assumptions behind the simulation and repeat the core model comparison. All methods will be trained and tested in chronological order and compared under the same false-alarm budget. Each alert will include its estimated risk, uncertainty, and main contributing factors.
Data
To provide a known ground truth for evaluation, we will use a One Health simulator covering multiple regions, resistance determinants, and years. It will generate realistic emergence events across the four surveillance sectors, including signals that disappear before reaching humans. Reporting delays, missing observations, unequal surveillance capacity, and transmission between regions will be included to reflect practical surveillance challenges.
To test whether the simulated relationships are supported by observed evidence, we will use three complementary real-world sources. NCBI Pathogen Detection provides dated isolates from human, animal, food, and environmental sources and supports cross-sector timing analyses and a real-data replication of the core comparison (National Center for Biotechnology Information, n.d.). Harmonised EFSA/ECDC reports support comparisons of animal and human resistance across countries (European Food Safety Authority & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2025). WHO GLASS provides national antimicrobial-consumption and resistance indicators for assessing whether changes in antimicrobial use tend to precede changes in resistance (World Health Organization, 2022).
The simulator and real data serve different purposes. Simulation allows warning time to be measured against known emergence events, whereas real data is used to examine whether the assumed cross-sector relationships are observable in existing surveillance systems.
Tools
The system will be implemented in Python. Pandas and NumPy will support data processing, temporal alignment, and simulation. Scikit-learn will be used for conventional baselines, gradient boosting, probability calibration, and model combination, while PyTorch will support the temporal and graph-based neural networks.
SHAP will be used to explain the factors contributing to individual alerts (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). SciPy will support statistical comparisons and uncertainty estimation. Data will be retrieved and processed with requests and openpyxl. Matplotlib will produce analytical figures, while Streamlit and Plotly will provide an interactive dashboard for presenting alerts and their supporting evidence.
Expected Results
The primary expected outcome is a proof-of-concept AI early-warning system that integrates One Health surveillance data and produces weekly, interpretable AMR risk alerts. A dashboard will show each alert, its estimated risk and uncertainty, and the evidence contributing to it.
The evaluation will show whether the system can identify emerging resistance earlier than clinical-only surveillance at the same false-alarm rate. Performance will be assessed using sensitivity, realised false-alarm rate, and lead time. Model comparisons and sector-ablation analyses will indicate whether additional sectors provide useful early signals and whether more complex models offer an advantage over simpler approaches.
The real-data analyses will indicate whether the relationships assumed by the simulator are supported by existing surveillance records. Because real data is less complete and has lower temporal resolution, the simulated advantage may not fully transfer. This comparison will therefore identify both the potential value of the system and the data improvements required for practical deployment.
Benefits
AMR-OHEWS could give public-health researchers, healthcare professionals, and agencies more time to investigate emerging resistance before it becomes widespread in clinical settings. Earlier warning could support targeted sampling, antimicrobial-stewardship reviews, supply-chain investigations, or wastewater and effluent controls.
By combining evidence across human, animal, food, and environmental sectors, the system could provide a more complete view of AMR risk than clinical surveillance alone. Its explicit false-alarm budget, uncertainty estimates, and alert explanations would support transparent, human-led decisions. The system is intended as a screening and prioritisation tool (World Health Organization, 2021).
Limitations
Uneven surveillance may make well-sampled regions appear riskier and poorly sampled regions safer. The dashboard will therefore show sampling intensity and testing denominators alongside alert rates. Alerts will support human-led investigation rather than automatic intervention, and each alert will display its estimated risk, uncertainty, and contributing inputs.
Simulation provides precise lead-time ground truth, but results remain sensitive to assumptions about transmission delays, fading signals, and spatial spread. Real-world data are also unevenly sampled and incomplete, while missingness, reporting delays, limited environmental data, and model drift may reduce deployment performance.
